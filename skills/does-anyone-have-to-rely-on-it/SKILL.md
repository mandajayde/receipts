# Test whether a proposed rating, standard or assurance product has anyone who must rely on it

## Inputs
- The proposed product in one paragraph: what is measured, who is assessed, who pays
- Who asked, and whether they want it to work
- The incumbents, if any

## Outputs
- A judgement on whether the asker's wish shaped your answer, made before the analysis rather than after
- Which of the three reliance categories the product achieves, if any
- Whether the thing being measured is a costly act or a drafted statement
- Whether the measure can discriminate, or is saturated
- A plain statement of what was established and what was only made plausible

## Steps
1. Check yourself first, because the request is shaped. 'Find an angle they have not considered' presupposes that one exists; 'is this a good business' presupposes an answer you can give. If the person asking wants the answer to be yes, write that down before you start, and get an independent reader — ideally on a different model, which does not share your priors — to answer one question before any substantive critique: is this motivated reasoning with sources attached? Measured 2026-09-14: three angles produced on request in one session, all three refuted; the reviewer found the same failure each time, and the author did not see it once.
2. Read the company's own public disclosure BEFORE advising them. Twice in one session an angle was contradicted by the subject's own website: a buyer described as 'nobody has named' was named in their own published article four months earlier, and a capability treated as a defensible position was described by the company itself as not yet issued, with the corpus still being assembled. If your advice is more confident than the subject's own disclosure about its own product, that is a finding about you.
3. Apply the three reliance questions, and accept that failing all three is the usual answer. Is anyone LEGALLY REQUIRED to obtain this assessment? Does a buyer or counterparty CONTRACTUALLY REQUIRE it? Does someone VOLUNTARILY RELY on it because it improves a consequential decision? General enthusiasm is not reliance, and neither is a testimonial. A product that achieves none of the three is consulting with a number attached, whatever else is true of it.
4. Ask whether the thing you would measure is a costly act or a drafted statement. This is the sharpest single test. The patent index that is usually cited as the precedent for rating intangibles ran on patent RENEWAL FEES — paying to keep a patent alive is an act, it costs money, and no lawyer can draft it. Corporate disclosure is the opposite: it is a statement drafted by counsel, and it says what the drafter wants. Find the renewal fee in your domain, or accept that you are scoring prose.
5. Test for saturation before assuming a public signal exists. A field that most of the universe populates cannot rank that universe. Measured 2026: 83% of the S&P 500 disclose AI as a risk, up from 12% in 2023, in a section courts themselves describe as boilerplate. The largest study of those filings, 30,000+ documents, calls them vague and boilerplate and concedes it has no method for measuring how boilerplate they are. Nobody has measured the informativeness of the signal; that absence is the finding.
6. Ask who is closer to the money than the assessor. Insurers, carriers, auditors and procurement departments sit between an assessment and a decision, and any of them can absorb the function by adding a question to a form. If the underwriter is already asking, an independent rating has to displace an incumbent rather than fill a gap. Check the incumbent before declaring the space empty.
7. Ask whether anyone has demonstrated predictive validity, and expect no. Credit ratings had to show they predicted default. In AI assurance, as of September 2026, no study, vendor dataset or academic work was found correlating pre-deployment scores with post-deployment incidents — including from the firm that underwrites insurance against its own standard. A score that predicts nothing may still describe something, but say which you are selling.
8. Check whether the measurement can survive being published. If the artefact is a standardised test, it eventually becomes a public test, and a public test gets recognised by the thing it measures. Evaluation awareness in frontier models is reported to scale with model size; one frontier model flagged public benchmarks as likely evaluations 19.8% of the time against 2.0% for internal ones. A private, rotating suite held by an assessor survives this; a published standard does not.
9. Separate what was established from what was made plausible, and say so in those words. The honest output of most of these reviews is: an unsettled implementation problem, not a demonstrated opportunity. Narrowing a claim after counterevidence is legitimate; keeping the original commercial conclusion after removing its premise is a rescue. If you narrowed, rebuild the support or withdraw the conclusion.

## Cautions
- The check on yourself is first for a reason. Every other step in this recipe was applied correctly in the session that produced it, and the author still generated three unsound angles, because the checks were run on the idea and not on the reason the idea was produced.
- Do not confuse an absent standard with unmet demand, and do not confuse a published standard with satisfied demand. Neither follows.
- Do not describe a firm as having accredited auditors because its marketing says so. Check who the accreditation body is; in one case examined here the firm accredits its own standard.
- A rating is not made useless by lacking accreditation, and judgement is not inherently unaccreditable — assessors exercise judgement against specified criteria routinely. The question is the scope and the scheme, not whether a human decides.
- This recipe kills proposals. That is its purpose and also its bias: it will not tell you when something is worth building, only when the case has not been made.
- Nothing confidential, privileged, or about a client. If in doubt, do not use this for the job.
